# Founder skills

The competencies this specific business demands, and the order in which to acquire them. It is
here because the constraint on Bitrate stopped being technical some time ago, and the gap
between "a strong engineer with a large project" and "someone running a company" is a real,
learnable, and specific set of skills.

The starting position is unusually strong on one axis and unstarted on the others. That is worth
naming plainly rather than treating every competency as equally urgent.

## The shift

> The code is no longer the deliverable.

A perfectly built Bitrate — good architecture, clean CI, real tests, a working deploy pipeline —
is worth approximately zero as a business if no artist gets value from it. The deliverables
become: artists who use it, artists who come back, releases completed, and money artists earn
because of it.

The progression, and each step is a real change in what the day looks like:

> Software Engineer → Product Engineer → Founder → CEO

## The competencies

Ordered by when they first bind, not by importance.

### 1. Product and market

Distinguishing a good feature from a problem someone will change their behaviour to solve.
Customer interviews, ICP, jobs-to-be-done, retention, cohorts.

The question is not "is our player better than Spotify's". It is **"why does an artist come
back to Bitrate for their second release?"** Everything else is downstream of that.

→ [Validation plan](./validation.md)

### 2. Music business

The most important domain skill here, and the one with no substitute. The `artist → distributor
→ DSP → listener` chain, masters versus compositions, royalties, PROs and CMOs, ISRC and UPC,
splits, distributor economics.

The bar is not "become a music lawyer". It is to talk to an artist, a distributor and a lawyer
without being led, and to recognise when something being said is wrong.

→ [Music business](./music-business.md)

### 3. Business model and unit economics

Where one euro of revenue comes from and what it costs to get. MRR, gross margin, CAC, LTV,
churn, ARPU, burn, runway.

The specific trap here: several revenue models are plausible at once — artist subscription,
distribution fee, marketplace commission, listener subscription, ads, B2B. Running more than one
early makes it impossible to learn which works.

→ [Business model](./business-model.md)

### 4. Sales and negotiation

Even for a consumer product, the first years are personal selling — to artists, distributors,
studios, hires, and eventually investors. Cold outreach, discovery calls, handling objections,
partnership and contract negotiation.

For an engineer this is usually the least comfortable item on the list and the one with the
highest immediate return. The first ten artists will not arrive on their own.

### 5. Marketing and growth

Positioning, channels, funnels, activation, referral loops, content, creator marketing.

The loop specific to this business:

> artist → release → content → listeners → results → case study → new artists

### 6. Finance

Not bookkeeping — reading a P&L, a balance sheet and a cash-flow statement, and building a
budget and a model. The working standard is being able to answer at any moment: *"€40k in the
bank, €8k monthly burn — about five months of runway."*

### 7. Legal and compliance

Enough to know when a decision cannot be made without a lawyer, which is a different skill from
knowing the law. IP, music licensing, GDPR, consumer law, contracts, payments, the AI Act.

→ [Law roadmap](./law-roadmap.md), which draws the
[buy-versus-learn line](./law-roadmap.md#what-to-buy-rather-than-learn) explicitly.

### 8. Fundraising

Cap table, dilution, valuation, SAFEs and convertibles, pre-seed and seed, the deck, due
diligence. Worth understanding well before it is needed, because learning it during a
negotiation is a losing position.

### 9. Leadership

Becomes real at the first five to ten people. Hiring, delegation, ownership, goal-setting,
feedback, letting people go, culture. The job changes from *"I build Bitrate"* to *"I build the
organisation that builds Bitrate."*

### 10. Strategy

Probably the hardest, and the one this project most needs. Saying **"no, not now"** to nine good
ideas for the sake of one.

The [backlog](./product-backlog.md) contains dozens of genuinely good ideas. That is exactly the
problem: without a strategy, a good idea is indistinguishable from a priority.

## Where to spend learning time

For the next 6–12 months, as a rough allocation of attention rather than a calendar:

| Area | Share |
|---|---|
| Product and users | 40% |
| Music industry | 20% |
| Marketing and sales | 15% |
| Finance | 10% |
| Legal | 10% |
| Fundraising | 5% |

The weighting reflects two things: product and users is where the answers are, and music
industry is the domain knowledge with no substitute and the longest lead time.

## Do not study first and start after

Bitrate itself is the fastest way to learn most of this. The concrete goals, in order, each
teaching more than a course would:

1. **Ten artists** who actually use Bitrate for a release.
2. **The first €100** of revenue.
3. **A hundred active artists.**

Each is a real milestone, each is reachable from where the project is now, and each forces the
competencies above in roughly the order listed. The
[concierge releases](./validation.md#the-rule) in particular teach product, music business, and
sales simultaneously, which is why they appear so early in the validation plan.

## What to buy rather than learn

Founder time is the binding constraint, and some of this is not learnable to a useful depth in
a reasonable time:

**Buy:** contract drafting, trademark work, company formation and accounting, VAT registration,
and anything touching regulated payments.

**Learn:** everything customer-facing, the music-industry vocabulary, and enough of the legal
and financial landscape to brief a professional well and to know when one is needed.
